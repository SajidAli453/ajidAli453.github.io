How to Split a Bill Fairly — A Complete Guide

The bill arrives, somebody picks it up, and the table goes quiet. Four friends, one total, and four different ideas about what "fair" means. One person had a salad, another had steak and two drinks, and somebody ordered a starter "for the table" that only two people touched. Splitting a bill fairly is not about being generous or being strict — it is about agreeing on one rule before the maths starts, and then doing the arithmetic exactly. This guide explains the three ways to split a bill, works through each one with real numbers, and shows how to handle tax, tips, and the awkward pennies that never divide evenly.

What "fair" actually means

There are three common definitions of a fair split, and most arguments come from two people using different ones without realising it. The first is the equal split: everybody pays the same amount, whatever they ordered. It is the simplest rule and the fairest when everyone ordered roughly the same — a group of friends sharing pizzas, or a family meal where nobody kept count. The second is the itemised split: everybody pays for exactly what they ordered, plus their share of anything shared. It is the fairest when orders differ a lot — one salad against a steak and two cocktails. The third is the proportional split: everybody pays in proportion to what they spent, which is how tax and tips are usually shared on top of an itemised split.

The rule to pick is the one the table agrees on before anyone starts dividing. An equal split agreed in advance is fair; an equal split imposed on the person who only had a salad is not. The guide to how averages work explains the idea behind the equal split: the bill total divided by the number of people is the mean share, and it is only fair when the mean is close to what each person actually had. If one person's order costs three times the mean, the table needs the itemised rule instead. Agree first, divide second — that order saves more friendships than any calculator.

Worked example 1: splitting equally

Four friends share a meal and the bill comes to £96.00. Nobody kept track of who ordered what, and the orders were similar, so they agree to split equally. The maths is one division: £96.00 ÷ 4 = £24.00 each. Everybody pays £24.00 and the bill is settled exactly, with no pennies left over.

Now add a tip. They agree on 10%, which is £96.00 × 0.10 = £9.60. The new total is £96.00 + £9.60 = £105.60, and £105.60 ÷ 4 = £26.40 each. The guide to how to calculate a percentage shows how to work out the tip itself, and the Percentage Calculator checks it in seconds. The important point is the order: add the tip to the bill first, then divide. Dividing first and tipping afterwards gives the same answer here, but only because the tip is a percentage of the whole bill — adding it first keeps the rounding in one place, which matters in the next examples.

Worked example 2: splitting by what each person ordered

Four friends order very different meals, so they split by what each person had. Ana has a main at £18.50 and a dessert at £6.00. Ben has a main at £22.00 and a drink at £4.50. Cara has a main at £15.00. Dan has a main at £19.75. They also shared a starter costing £12.00, which they agree to split equally four ways: £12.00 ÷ 4 = £3.00 each.

Add each person's own items, then add the £3.00 share of the starter. Ana pays £18.50 + £6.00 + £3.00 = £27.50. Ben pays £22.00 + £4.50 + £3.00 = £29.50. Cara pays £15.00 + £3.00 = £18.00. Dan pays £19.75 + £3.00 = £22.75. Check the total: £27.50 + £29.50 + £18.00 + £22.75 = £97.75, and the food itself was £18.50 + £6.00 + £22.00 + £4.50 + £15.00 + £19.75 + £12.00 = £97.75, so the shares add up exactly. The check matters — if the shares do not add up to the bill, something has been missed or double-counted, and it is far better to find that out at the table than after everyone has paid.

Worked example 3: adding tax and tip fairly

Suppose the food bill is £120.00 and the restaurant adds a 12.5% service charge. The service charge is £120.00 × 0.125 = £15.00, so the total is £120.00 + £15.00 = £135.00. If five people split equally, each pays £135.00 ÷ 5 = £27.00. That is the easy case.

On an itemised split, the tip must be shared in proportion to what each person spent, not equally. Say Ana's food came to £30.00 out of the £120.00 total. Her share of the food is £30.00 ÷ £120.00 = 0.25, or one quarter, so her share of the £15.00 service charge is 0.25 × £15.00 = £3.75, and her total is £30.00 + £3.75 = £33.75. The same rule works for everyone: your fraction of the food bill, multiplied by the tip, is your share of the tip. The Average Calculator is handy here for the equal-split case, but for the itemised case the proportional rule is the fair one — an equal share of the tip would make the lightest eater subsidise the heaviest eater's tip as well as their food.

Worked example 4: the rounding problem

Bills rarely divide neatly. Three friends split a bill of £47.50 equally. The division gives £47.50 ÷ 3 = £15.8333…, which is not a real amount of money — nobody can pay a third of a penny. The fix is to give two people £15.83 and one person £15.84: £15.83 + £15.83 + £15.84 = £47.50 exactly. The person paying the extra penny rotates next time, or the group agrees that the extra penny goes to whoever suggested the restaurant.

The same trick works for any split. Four friends splitting £100.00 pay £25.00 each with no problem, but three friends splitting £100.00 face £33.333… each, so two pay £33.33 and one pays £33.34: £33.33 + £33.33 + £33.34 = £100.00. The rule is simple: round all but one share down to the penny, and give the last person whatever is left so the shares add up to the bill exactly. Never round every share up — four shares of £25.01 add up to £100.04, which is 4p more than the bill, and somebody has overpaid for nothing.

Common mistakes to avoid

1. Splitting the food bill but forgetting the tip and tax. A £96.00 bill with a 10% tip is £105.60, not £96.00. Agree the total first — food plus tax plus tip — and only then divide. Dividing the food and adding the tip afterwards usually ends with somebody short.

2. Splitting equally when orders differ wildly. An equal split is fair only when orders are similar. If one person had a £15.00 salad and another had a £40.00 steak with drinks, the equal split charges the salad eater for food they never had. Use the itemised split when the gap is large.

3. Charging everyone for a shared item nobody agreed to share. A starter "for the table" is shared only if the table agreed. If one person ordered it for themselves, it belongs on their share, not everyone's. Shared items need a shared decision first.

4. Rounding every share up. Rounding £15.833… up to £15.84 for all three people collects £47.52 for a £47.50 bill. Round down for all but one person and let the last share absorb the pennies, so the total is exact.

5. Letting one person pay and never settling up. The person who puts the whole bill on their card is lending the table money, not treating it. Settle the same evening, with the exact shares worked out — a photo of the bill and a message with each person's amount takes a minute and stops the debt from becoming a grudge.

Frequently asked questions

What if one person only had a salad while everyone else had three courses? Use the itemised split: they pay for their salad plus their share of anything genuinely shared, such as the bread or the starter everyone ate. An equal split would charge them for courses they never ordered, and that is the most common cause of bill arguments.

What about the person who does not drink? Drinks are almost always the biggest source of unfairness, because a round of cocktails costs far more than a glass of water. Put drinks on the itemised split even when the food is split equally — each person pays for their own drinks, and the food is shared. The same applies to an expensive bottle of wine ordered by two people: it is theirs, not the table's.

Should the tip be split equally or in proportion? In proportion to what each person spent, when the split is itemised — your share of the tip matches your share of the food. When the split is equal, the tip is equal too, because everyone is already paying the same amount. Mixing the two rules is how somebody ends up paying a tip on somebody else's steak.

Is there a quick way to do this without an app? Yes. For an equal split, divide the total by the number of people — that is the mean, and it takes one division. For an itemised split, add up each person's own items first, then add shared items divided equally, then add the proportional tip. The percentage guide covers the tip maths, and the whole job fits on the back of the receipt.

Splitting a bill fairly comes down to two steps in the right order: agree the rule, then do the maths. Equal when orders are similar, itemised when they differ, proportional for the tip, and the pennies settled so the shares add up to the bill exactly. With that, the bill stops being the awkward end of the evening and becomes one more division — done in a minute, and fair to everyone at the table.
