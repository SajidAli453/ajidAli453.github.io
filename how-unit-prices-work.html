Unit Prices: How to Spot the Better Deal — A Complete Guide

Two packs sit side by side on the shelf. One costs £2.00, the other £1.20 — so the cheaper one is the better deal, surely? Not always. The £2.00 pack may hold twice as much, or the £1.20 pack may be on offer and actually cheaper per gram. The only fair way to compare packs of different sizes is the unit price: what one fixed amount — 100 grams, one litre, one item — actually costs. This guide explains how to work it out in seconds, how to compare packs that use different units, and the traps that catch shoppers out.

What a unit price is

A unit price is the price of one unit of something: price ÷ quantity. If a 500-gram pack of rice costs £1.20, its unit price is £1.20 ÷ 500 grams. That gives a tiny number, £0.0024 per gram, which is hard to use — so shops and shoppers usually scale it up to a friendlier unit, such as the price per 100 grams. For the rice, £1.20 ÷ 5 hundreds of grams = £0.24 per 100 grams. The formula never changes: unit price = price ÷ quantity, then scale both packs to the same unit before comparing.

Pick the unit that fits what you are buying. Food is usually compared per 100 grams (or per kilogram), drinks per litre (or per 100 millilitres), and counted things — eggs, toilet rolls, batteries — per item. The rule is simple and strict: both packs must be measured in the same unit. Comparing a price per 100 grams with a price per kilogram is like comparing a speed in km/h with a speed in m/s — the numbers look comparable and mean different things. The guide to how ratios and proportions work explains the scaling behind this: doubling the quantity doubles the fair price, and halving it halves it.

Worked example 1: rice by weight

A 500-gram bag of rice costs £1.20 and a 1-kilogram bag costs £2.00. Which is the better deal? Work out the price per 100 grams for each. The small bag holds 5 hundreds of grams, so £1.20 ÷ 5 = £0.24 per 100 grams. The large bag holds 10 hundreds of grams, so £2.00 ÷ 10 = £0.20 per 100 grams. The large bag is cheaper per 100 grams, by 4p, even though it costs more at the till.

Check it by working backwards: at £0.20 per 100 grams, 10 hundreds of grams cost 10 × £0.20 = £2.00, which matches the large bag, and at £0.24 per 100 grams, 5 hundreds cost 5 × £0.24 = £1.20, which matches the small bag. The saving is real but modest: buying the large bag instead of two small bags costs £2.00 instead of £2.40, a saving of 40p. That is the whole point of a unit price — it turns a vague feeling about value into an exact number.

Worked example 2: juice by volume

A 1-litre carton of juice costs £1.80 and a 2-litre carton costs £3.00. Here the unit is the litre, so the division is easy: the small carton is £1.80 per litre, and the large carton is £3.00 ÷ 2 = £1.50 per litre. The large carton is cheaper by 30p per litre.

Put it in shopping terms: two 1-litre cartons would cost 2 × £1.80 = £3.60 for the same 2 litres, while the 2-litre carton costs £3.00 — a saving of 60p. Watch out for the opposite trap with volumes: a 750-millilitre bottle is 0.75 litres, not 1 litre. If it costs £1.50, its price per litre is £1.50 ÷ 0.75 = £2.00 per litre, which is dearer than the £1.80 carton even though £1.50 looks cheaper on the shelf. Convert millilitres to litres first — divide by 1,000 — or work per 100 millilitres instead: £1.50 ÷ 7.5 = £0.20 per 100 millilitres, against £0.18 per 100 millilitres for the 1-litre carton.

Worked example 3: different sizes, same unit

Sometimes neither pack is a round number. A 200-gram jar of coffee costs £3.00 and a 450-gram jar costs £6.30. Work per 100 grams again. The small jar holds 2 hundreds of grams, so £3.00 ÷ 2 = £1.50 per 100 grams. The large jar holds 4.5 hundreds of grams, so £6.30 ÷ 4.5 = £1.40 per 100 grams. The large jar wins by 10p per 100 grams.

The check works backwards: 4.5 × £1.40 = £6.30, which matches the large jar exactly, and 2 × £1.50 = £3.00, which matches the small one. A useful shortcut when the numbers are awkward is to scale one pack up to the other's size. Scale the small jar to 450 grams: £3.00 × (450 ÷ 200) = £3.00 × 2.25 = £6.75 for 450 grams, against the large jar's £6.30 — the same answer, 45p cheaper for the same weight. The guide to how estimating works shows how to do this kind of scaling roughly in your head before you commit to the exact division.

Worked example 4: price per item, and scaling to the same quantity

For counted items, the unit is one item. A box of 6 eggs costs £1.50, so each egg costs £1.50 ÷ 6 = £0.25, or 25p. A box of 12 eggs costs £2.40, so each egg costs £2.40 ÷ 12 = £0.20, or 20p. The 12-box is cheaper per egg, and buying 12 eggs as two 6-boxes would cost 2 × £1.50 = £3.00 instead of £2.40 — a saving of 60p.

The same idea works when the packs are hard to compare directly. A 1.2-kilogram box of washing powder costs £4.80 and a 2-kilogram box costs £7.00. Per kilogram, the small box is £4.80 ÷ 1.2 = £4.00 per kilogram and the large box is £7.00 ÷ 2 = £3.50 per kilogram, so the large box is cheaper. Or scale the small box up to 2 kilograms: £4.80 × (2 ÷ 1.2) = £4.80 × 5/3 = £8.00 for 2 kilograms, against £7.00 — a saving of £1.00 on the same amount. Either route gives the same verdict; pick the one whose division looks easier.

When the bigger pack loses

Bigger is not always cheaper, and offers are where the unit price earns its keep. A 500-gram pack of pasta on offer costs £0.90, while the regular 1-kilogram pack costs £2.20. Per 100 grams, the small pack is £0.90 ÷ 5 = £0.18 and the large pack is £2.20 ÷ 10 = £0.22. This time the smaller pack wins by 4p per 100 grams — the offer has flipped the usual pattern. Buying two small packs costs £1.80 for a kilogram, 40p less than the large pack.

That is why a unit price is worth working out even when a pack shouts "value" or "family size" on the front: the words on the pack are advertising, and the unit price is arithmetic. It also helps with genuine discounts. If a pack is reduced by a percentage, the unit price falls by the same percentage — a 25% discount turns £0.20 per 100 grams into £0.15 — and the guide to how to calculate a percentage explains how to work out the reduced price first, then divide as usual. Common mistakes to avoid

1. Comparing different units. A price per 100 grams and a price per kilogram differ by a factor of 10: £0.24 per 100 grams is £2.40 per kilogram. Convert both packs to the same unit before comparing, or the cheaper-looking number may be the dearer pack.

2. Comparing the shelf price instead of the unit price. The till price tells you what you pay today; only the unit price tells you what you pay for the same amount. A £3.00 pack can be better value than a £1.20 pack if it holds far more than two and a half times as much.

3. Forgetting the pack size is not the amount you use. A 2-litre bottle is only a bargain if you will use 2 litres before it goes off. Unit price measures value per amount bought, not per amount used — food that is thrown away costs its full price for nothing, however low its unit price was.

4. Rounding before comparing. Unit prices often run to three decimal places, such as £0.237 per 100 grams. If two packs are close, rounding both to the nearest penny can make them look equal when one is really cheaper. Keep an extra digit while you compare, and round only for the final answer.

5. Trusting "value", "economy" or "family" on the label. Those words are not measured or regulated as a promise of the lowest unit price. Work out the unit price yourself — it takes one division, and it is the only claim on the pack that cannot be dressed up.

Frequently asked questions

What unit should I use — per 100 grams or per kilogram? Use whichever the shop uses, so you can compare with the shelf label — in most supermarkets that is per 100 grams for food and per litre for drinks. Per kilogram is the same comparison multiplied by 10, so the winner never changes; only the numbers get bigger. For counted items, use per item.

How do I compare a pack in grams with one in ounces? Convert first: 1 ounce is about 28.35 grams, so a 16-ounce pack is about 16 × 28.35 = 453.6 grams. Work out each pack's price per 100 grams after converting, and compare those. Mixing grams and ounces without converting is the same error as mixing minutes and hours.

Does the unit price include a discount? Yes, if you use the price you actually pay. Work out the discounted price first — a 25% reduction on £2.00 leaves £1.50 — and then divide by the quantity. A "3 for 2" offer works the same way: three packs for the price of two means you pay for 2 and get 3, so divide the price of two packs by the quantity in all three.

When is the cheapest unit price the wrong choice? When you will not use it all, when storage or weight matters, or when quality differs — a cheaper per-gram coffee you do not enjoy is not a saving. The unit price answers one question only: which pack gives you more of the same thing for your money. The rest of the decision is yours.

Unit prices reward one habit above all: divide before you decide. Price ÷ quantity, both packs in the same unit, and the better deal is the smaller number — whether that is the big pack, the small pack, or the one on offer. With that habit, the shelf stops being a guessing game, and the "value" pack has to prove itself with arithmetic, not with bigger writing.
