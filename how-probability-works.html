How Probability Works — A Complete Guide

Every day you judge chances without thinking about it: will it rain this afternoon, will the bus come in the next five minutes, will your team win on Saturday? Probability is the maths of those judgements. It turns a vague feeling — "probably", "unlikely", "almost certain" — into a number you can work with, compare, and check. This guide explains what probability means, how to work it out in three everyday situations, and the mistakes that catch people out.

What probability means

The probability of something happening is a number between 0 and 1. A probability of 0 means it cannot happen. A probability of 1 means it is certain to happen. Everything else sits in between: the closer the number is to 1, the more likely the event is. You will also see probability written as a percentage: multiply by 100, so a probability of 0.5 is 50%, and a probability of 0.25 is 25%.

For a fair, simple situation, the rule is easy: probability equals the number of favourable outcomes divided by the total number of possible outcomes. Toss a fair coin and there are two possible outcomes, heads and tails, so the probability of heads is 1 out of 2, written 1/2 or 0.5 or 50%. The word "fair" matters: it means every outcome is equally likely. A coin that is bent, or a die with one face heavier than the rest, breaks the rule, because the outcomes are no longer equally likely.

The probability scale

It helps to picture probabilities on a scale from 0 to 1. At 0 sits the impossible: a coin landing on its edge and staying there, for practical purposes. At 1 sits the certain: the sun rising tomorrow. In the middle, 0.5 is the even chance — as likely to happen as not, like a fair coin toss. A probability of 0.25 is unlikely but far from impossible: it happens about one time in four. A probability of 0.75 is likely: it happens about three times in four, but the fourth time still surprises you.

One habit makes the scale useful: the probabilities of all the possible outcomes must add up to 1, or 100%. If you list every way a die can land — 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 and 6 — each has a probability of 1/6, and six lots of 1/6 add up to exactly 1. If your probabilities add up to more or less than 1, something is wrong: an outcome is missing, one is counted twice, or the total is wrong. That single check catches more probability errors than any other habit. Worked example 1: a fair coin and a fair die

Start with the two simplest chance devices. A fair coin has two outcomes, heads and tails, so the probability of heads is 1 ÷ 2 = 1/2, which is 0.5 or 50%. Toss it 100 times and you should expect about 50 heads — about, not exactly. Probability describes the long run, not the next toss: ten heads in a row is unusual, but it does not make tails any more likely on the eleventh toss. Each toss starts fresh at 1/2.

A fair die has six outcomes, 1 to 6, each with probability 1/6, about 16.7%. Some questions need counting. The probability of rolling an even number — 2, 4 or 6 — is 3 favourable outcomes out of 6, which is 3/6 = 1/2, or 50%. The probability of rolling more than 4 — that is, 5 or 6 — is 2 out of 6, which is 2/6 = 1/3, about 33.3%. The probability of rolling less than 3 — 1 or 2 — is also 2 out of 6, or 1/3. Notice the pattern: count the outcomes you want, count all the outcomes, and divide.

Worked example 2: marbles in a bag

A bag holds 10 marbles: 5 red, 3 blue and 2 green. You close your eyes and pick one marble without looking. First, check the total: 5 + 3 + 2 = 10 marbles, which matches the bag. Now work out each probability step by step. Picking a red marble is 5 ÷ 10 = 1/2, or 50%. Picking a blue marble is 3 ÷ 10 = 30%. Picking a green marble is 2 ÷ 10 = 1/5, or 20%. Now the check: 50% + 30% + 20% = 100%, exactly as it must be.

The same bag answers a second kind of question: the probability of something not happening. The probability of not picking a red marble is 1 − 1/2 = 1/2, or 50%, because 5 of the 10 marbles are not red. In general, the probability of "not" is 1 minus the probability of the event itself. A weather forecast that gives a 30% chance of rain is also giving you a 70% chance of no rain, since 100% − 30% = 70%.

Worked example 3: two dice and a raffle

Some events need two things to happen together. Roll two fair dice and ask for the probability that both show 6. There are 36 equally likely pairs — 6 results on the first die times 6 on the second — and only one of them is (6, 6). So the probability is 1/36, about 2.8%. The rule is to multiply: 1/6 × 1/6 = 1/36. This works when the two events are independent, meaning the first does not change the second: the first die cannot influence the second.

A raffle works the same way with one draw. There are 200 tickets and you hold 5 of them, so your probability of winning is 5 ÷ 200 = 1/40, or 2.5%. If you held 20 tickets instead, it would be 20 ÷ 200 = 1/10, or 10% — four times as many tickets, four times the chance. And if a friend holds 10 tickets, your chances together are 5 + 10 = 15 tickets out of 200, which is 15 ÷ 200 = 7.5%. Adding tickets adds chances, because each ticket is a separate favourable outcome.

Common mistakes to avoid

1. The gambler's fallacy. After five heads in a row, tails feels "due". It is not. A fair coin has no memory, and the next toss is still 1/2 heads. Short runs mislead; only the long run evens out, and even then only approximately.

2. Adding probabilities that overlap. The probability of rolling a 6 or an even number on one die is not 1/6 + 3/6 = 4/6. The 6 is already counted among the even numbers, so adding counts it twice. The correct answer is just 3/6 = 1/2. Add probabilities only when the outcomes cannot happen together.

3. Assuming everything is equally likely. A spinner with one big slice and one small slice does not give each slice the same chance. Count outcomes only when each one is equally likely; when they are not, work from the sizes or the weights instead.

4. Confusing probability with odds. Odds of 1 to 5 mean 1 favourable outcome against 5 unfavourable ones, so the probability is 1 out of 6, about 16.7% — not 1 out of 5. Bookmakers and betting slips use odds; the probability needs the total, which is 1 + 5 = 6.

5. Forgetting the probabilities must add to 1. If your answers for every outcome add to 97% or 103%, go back and check. An outcome is missing, one is counted twice, or the total is wrong. The same check that protects pie charts protects probability.

Frequently asked questions

Can a probability be more than 1? No. A probability of 1 means certain, and nothing can be more than certain. If your working gives 1.4 or 140%, you have added probabilities that overlap or used the wrong total. Go back to the count of favourable outcomes and the count of all outcomes.

What does a "30% chance of rain" actually mean? Roughly this: on 100 days with weather conditions like today's, it rains on about 30 of them. It does not mean it will rain for 30% of the day, and it does not mean rain is certain anywhere. It is a long-run frequency, like the coin that lands heads about half the time.

What is the difference between independent and dependent events? Independent events do not affect each other, like two dice. Dependent events do: draw a marble from the bag and do not put it back, and the next draw changes. With 5 red and 3 blue marbles, drawing a red first leaves 7 marbles, so the probability of blue next is 3 ÷ 7, about 42.9% — not the 3 ÷ 8 it was before the draw.

Why do games of chance always favour the house in the long run? Because the payouts are set slightly below what the probabilities would pay fairly. Each single bet can be won, but over thousands of bets the small gap between the true probability and the payout adds up in the house's favour. Probability explains the long run; it never predicts the next single result.

Probability rewards one habit above all: count carefully before you divide. Count the outcomes you want, count all the outcomes, check they add up to the whole — and remember that a probability describes the long run, not the next toss, roll or draw. With that habit, a coin, a die, a bag of marbles or a weather forecast will all tell you an honest number, and you will spot the claims that do not.
