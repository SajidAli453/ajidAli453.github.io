How Averages Work — Mean, Median, Mode and When to Use Each One

The word average is used every day, but it actually covers several different ideas, and choosing the wrong one can mislead you. The mean, the median and the mode are the three main averages, and each answers a slightly different question. The mean is the total divided by how many values there are. The median is the middle value when values are sorted. The mode is the most common value. In many everyday situations they are close together, but when data is uneven, they can tell very different stories, and knowing which one to use is a genuinely useful skill.

The mean: the ordinary average

To find the mean, add all the values and divide by the count. For test scores of 70, 80, 90 and 100, the sum is 340, the count is 4, and the mean is 85. The mean uses every value, which is both its strength and its weakness: it reflects the whole set, but a single extreme value can pull it a long way. If a fifth score of 40 is added, the sum becomes 380, the count becomes 5, and the mean falls to 76, even though four of the five scores are 70 or above. That sensitivity is why the mean alone can be misleading for incomes, house prices and other data where a few very large values exist. The median: the middle value

To find the median, sort the values from smallest to largest and pick the middle one. For 70, 80, 90 and 100, the median is the average of the two middle values, 80 and 90, which is 85. For 40, 70, 80, 90 and 100, the median is 80, the third of five values. The median is resistant to outliers: changing the largest value from 100 to 1,000 would not change the median at all, while it would raise the mean sharply. That is why house prices are often reported as a median: a few mansions should not make a typical home look unaffordable, and a few very cheap homes should not make it look cheap. When someone says “the typical value”, they often mean the median, even if they say “average”.

The mode: the most common value

The mode is the value that appears most often. In shoe sizes, survey answers or favourite colours, the mode is often the most useful average, because it tells you what is typical in the sense of most frequent, not most central. A shop deciding which sizes to stock cares about the mode, not the mean size, which might be a size nobody wears. Some data sets have no mode, if every value appears once, and some have two modes, if two values tie for most frequent. The mode can also be misleading on its own: in the scores 70, 70, 80, 90 and 100, the mode is 70, but that hides the higher scores, so it should be reported with the mean or median, not instead of them.

Worked examples

Consider weekly wages in a small team: 400, 450, 500, 550 and 5,000. The mean is 1,380, which suggests a well-paid team, but the median is 500, which describes four of the five people far better. The mean is pulled up by one very high wage, probably a manager or owner, and reporting only the mean would mislead a jobseeker. Consider instead exam marks: 60, 65, 70, 75 and 80. The mean is 70 and the median is 70, so either describes the group well, because the marks are evenly spread. Consider house prices in a street: most homes cost around 200,000, but one costs 2,000,000. The median stays near 200,000, while the mean jumps, so estate agents and journalists usually quote the median for “typical” prices.

Which average should you use?

Use the mean when every value matters equally and there are no extreme outliers, for example for average test scores in a class, average daily temperature over a month, or average speed where total distance and total time are known. Use the median when data is skewed, when there are outliers, or when you want a typical value that is not dragged by extremes, for example for incomes, house prices and waiting times. Use the mode when you care about the most common category, for example for sizes, ratings, or survey choices. In reports, the honest approach is often to give more than one average, plus the range: “the mean is 76, the median is 80, and scores ranged from 40 to 100” tells a fuller story than any single number, and it invites the reader to judge for themselves.

Common mistakes

A common mistake is averaging averages without weighting: if one class of 10 students averages 80 and another class of 30 students averages 60, the overall average is not 70, because the second class is three times larger. The correct overall mean weights by count: (10 × 80 + 30 × 60) ÷ 40 = 65. Another mistake is mixing units, such as averaging speeds over equal distances as if they were equal times, which needs a different calculation. A third mistake is treating an average as if it described everyone: an average income of 50,000 does not mean most people earn near 50,000, and an average temperature of 20 degrees does not mean every day was pleasant. Always ask for the spread, the median, and the count alongside an average, and be suspicious of any claim that gives you only one of them.

Averages are powerful because they compress a whole set of numbers into one, but that compression always loses information. The skill is not just calculating the mean, median or mode, but knowing which one answers your question, checking for outliers, and reporting enough context for others to judge. With those habits, averages become a tool for clear thinking rather than a way to hide inconvenient detail, and you will be much harder to mislead by a single, carefully chosen number.
