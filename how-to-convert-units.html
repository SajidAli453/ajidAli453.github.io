How to Convert Units — A Complete Guide

A recipe calls for 750 grams of flour, but your scales only show kilograms. A road sign says the next town is 5 kilometres away, and you want to know how many metres that is. A friend in America says it is 68 degrees outside, and you have no idea whether to wear a coat. Converting units is one of the most useful pieces of everyday maths: the same quantity, written in a different unit. This guide explains the metric system, the imperial units you still meet, and the two-step method — find the conversion factor, then multiply or divide — that works for every conversion, with worked examples you can check line by line.

The metric system: multiply or divide by 10, 100 or 1,000

Metric units are built on powers of 10, which makes them easy to convert. For length: 1 kilometre = 1,000 metres, 1 metre = 100 centimetres, and 1 centimetre = 10 millimetres. For mass: 1 kilogram = 1,000 grams. For volume: 1 litre = 1,000 millilitres. The pattern never changes — moving to a smaller unit means multiplying, and moving to a larger unit means dividing. To change 5 kilometres into metres, multiply by 1,000: 5 × 1,000 = 5,000 metres. To change 750 grams into kilograms, divide by 1,000: 750 ÷ 1,000 = 0.75 kilograms. The decimal point simply moves: multiplying by 1,000 moves it three places to the right, dividing by 1,000 moves it three places to the left. The guide to how fractions, decimals and percentages relate explains why moving the decimal point works — it is the same idea as multiplying a decimal by 10, 100 or 1,000.

The prefixes tell you the factor. "Kilo" means 1,000, "centi" means one hundredth, and "milli" means one thousandth — so a kilometre is 1,000 metres, a centimetre is one hundredth of a metre, and a millilitre is one thousandth of a litre. Once you know the three prefixes, you can convert any metric unit without memorising a table: the factor is always 10, 100 or 1,000, and the only question is whether to multiply or divide. Ask yourself which unit is bigger. If you are going from a big unit to a small one, the number must get bigger — multiply. If you are going from a small unit to a big one, the number must get smaller — divide. If your answer goes the wrong way, you have mixed up the direction, and that one check catches most conversion errors.

Worked example 1: kilometres to metres and back

A runner trains for a 5-kilometre race and wants to know the distance in metres. One kilometre is 1,000 metres, and metres are the smaller unit, so multiply: 5 × 1,000 = 5,000 metres. The same race in centimetres would be 5,000 × 100 = 500,000 centimetres — a huge number, which is why nobody measures a race in centimetres.

Going the other way, a walk of 2,500 metres is 2,500 ÷ 1,000 = 2.5 kilometres. The check is quick: 2.5 kilometres is 2 kilometres and 500 metres, which is 2,000 + 500 = 2,500 metres, so the answer is right. Notice the shortcut for metric conversions: dividing by 1,000 moves the decimal point three places left (2,500 → 2.5), and multiplying by 1,000 moves it three places right (2.5 → 2,500). The guide to how estimating works is useful here: before you do the exact conversion, estimate — 2,500 metres is between 2 and 3 kilometres, so 2.5 kilometres looks right, while 25 kilometres would be an obvious mistake.

Worked example 2: grams, kilograms, litres and millilitres in a recipe

A recipe needs 750 grams of flour and 250 millilitres of milk. The flour is weighed in kilograms on the shop scales, and the milk jug is marked in litres. Convert the flour first: grams to kilograms is a move to a bigger unit, so divide by 1,000 — 750 ÷ 1,000 = 0.75 kilograms. The milk is 250 millilitres into litres: again divide by 1,000 — 250 ÷ 1,000 = 0.25 litres. So the recipe needs 0.75 kilograms of flour and 0.25 litres of milk.

Check by converting back. Half a kilogram is 500 grams, and 0.75 kilograms is three quarters of a kilogram, so 0.75 × 1,000 = 750 grams — right. A quarter of a litre is 250 millilitres, so 0.25 × 1,000 = 250 millilitres — right. The same method works for any kitchen conversion: 2,500 grams of potatoes is 2.5 kilograms, and 1.5 litres of water is 1,500 millilitres. The trap to avoid is mixing up the direction: 750 grams is 0.75 kilograms, not 750 kilograms — a bag of flour that heavy would need a forklift.

Worked example 3: imperial units — miles to kilometres

Imperial units do not use powers of 10, so the conversion factor must be remembered or looked up. One mile is 1.609 kilometres, and one inch is 2.54 centimetres. A 3-mile walk is 3 × 1.609 = 4.827 kilometres, about 4.83 kilometres. Going the other way, a 10-kilometre run is 10 ÷ 1.609 = 6.214 miles, about 6.21 miles — a 10K race is just over 6 miles, which matches what runners know.

For height, convert feet and inches to centimetres. One foot is 12 inches, and one inch is 2.54 centimetres. A person who is 5 feet 6 inches tall is 5 × 12 + 6 = 66 inches, and 66 × 2.54 = 167.64 centimetres, about 168 centimetres. The check: 66 inches is about 5 and a half feet, and 5.5 × 30.48 = 167.64, since one foot is 30.48 centimetres — the same answer by a second route. The guide to ratios and proportions explains the method behind this: converting units is a proportion — the ratio between the two units stays fixed, so multiplying by the factor scales the number correctly.

Worked example 4: temperature and speed

Temperature conversion is different because the two scales start at different points — it is not a simple multiplication. To change Celsius to Fahrenheit, multiply by 9/5 and add 32. A day of 20°C is 20 × 9/5 + 32 = 36 + 32 = 68°F, so your American friend's 68 degrees is a pleasant 20 degrees to you. Water boils at 100°C, which is 100 × 9/5 + 32 = 180 + 32 = 212°F. To go back, subtract 32 and multiply by 5/9: 68°F is (68 − 32) × 5/9 = 36 × 5/9 = 20°C.

Speed converts like any other rate: convert the distance and the time separately. A car travelling at 90 kilometres per hour is going 90,000 metres per hour, and one hour is 3,600 seconds, so 90,000 ÷ 3,600 = 25 metres per second. The check: 25 metres per second for 3,600 seconds is 90,000 metres, which is 90 kilometres — right. The same idea works for area and volume, where the factor is squared or cubed: 1 square metre is 10,000 square centimetres (100 × 100), so a table of 2.5 square metres is 25,000 square centimetres, and 1 cubic metre is 1,000,000 cubic centimetres. Square the factor for area, cube it for volume — that is the step people most often miss.

Common mistakes to avoid

1. Multiplying when you should divide. Converting 750 grams to kilograms gives 0.75, not 750,000. Before you calculate, ask which unit is bigger: if you are moving to a bigger unit, the number gets smaller, so divide. If your answer is bigger than you started with when it should be smaller, the direction is wrong.

2. Mixing up centimetres and millimetres. There are 10 millimetres in a centimetre, not 100. A nail 50 millimetres long is 5 centimetres, not 0.5 centimetres. The factor of 10 between neighbouring metric units is easy to lose track of when several conversions happen at once — convert one step at a time and write each step down.

3. Using the wrong conversion factor for imperial units. A mile is 1.609 kilometres, not 1.6 exactly, and a pound is 453.6 grams, not 500. Rounding the factor is fine for an estimate, but for an exact answer use the full factor and round only at the end, or the error grows with the size of the number.

4. Forgetting to square or cube the factor for area and volume. One metre is 100 centimetres, but one square metre is 100 × 100 = 10,000 square centimetres, not 100. This is the most common error in area conversions, and it makes answers wrong by a factor of 100.

5. Converting temperature by multiplying alone. Celsius and Fahrenheit do not share a zero point, so 20°C is not 20 × 9/5 = 36°F — it is 68°F, because you must add 32. Always use the full formula, in both directions.

Frequently asked questions

How do I remember whether to multiply or divide? Ask which unit is bigger. Big unit to small unit means more of the small units, so multiply. Small unit to big unit means fewer of the big units, so divide. Kilometres are bigger than metres, so 5 kilometres becomes 5,000 metres (multiply); grams are smaller than kilograms, so 750 grams becomes 0.75 kilograms (divide). The size check takes two seconds and catches the most common error.

What is the quickest metric shortcut? Move the decimal point. Multiplying by 10, 100 or 1,000 moves it 1, 2 or 3 places to the right; dividing moves it the same number of places to the left. So 2.5 kilometres is 2,500 metres (three places right), and 750 grams is 0.75 kilograms (three places left). No long multiplication is needed for metric conversions at all.

How many grams are in a pound? One pound is 453.6 grams, usually rounded to 454 grams. So 2 pounds is about 907 grams — just under a kilogram. The reverse is handy too: 1 kilogram is about 2.2 pounds, so a 5-kilogram bag is about 11 pounds. For cooking, the rounded values are accurate enough; for science or medicine, use the full factor.

Why do some conversions need two steps? Because the units measure different things, or the factor is not direct. Converting miles per hour to metres per second needs the distance (miles to metres) and the time (hours to seconds) converted separately. Converting square metres to square centimetres needs the length factor squared. Break the problem into steps, convert each part, and check the final number against a rough estimate.

Converting units comes down to one method: find the factor, check the direction, and do the arithmetic. Metric conversions are decimal-point moves, imperial conversions are multiplications by a fixed factor, and temperature, area and volume each have one extra step to remember. With that method, a recipe, a road sign and a weather report all speak the same language — yours.
