How Speed, Distance and Time Work — A Complete Guide

How long will the drive take? How far can you cycle in an hour? How fast was that train really going? Speed, distance and time answer all three questions, and they are tied together by one simple relationship: if you know any two of them, you can work out the third. This guide explains the three formulas, how to switch between units without tripping up, and the mistakes that catch people out.

The three formulas in one triangle

Speed is how fast something moves: the distance it covers in one unit of time. The definition gives the first formula: speed = distance ÷ time. A car that covers 240 kilometres in 3 hours travels at 240 ÷ 3 = 80 kilometres per hour. The other two formulas are the same relationship rearranged. Distance = speed × time: at 80 kilometres per hour for 3 hours, the car covers 80 × 3 = 240 kilometres. Time = distance ÷ speed: 240 kilometres at 80 kilometres per hour takes 240 ÷ 80 = 3 hours.

A handy way to remember all three is the triangle: write D at the top and S and T side by side underneath. Cover the letter you want and the triangle shows the formula: cover S and you see D over T, so speed = distance ÷ time; cover D and you see S next to T, so distance = speed × time; cover T and you see D over S, so time = distance ÷ speed. The triangle never changes; only the numbers you put into it do. Units: the part everyone forgets

Speed is always a distance unit divided by a time unit, and the two must match. Kilometres per hour (km/h) goes with kilometres and hours; metres per second (m/s) goes with metres and seconds. The common trap is mixing them: a distance in kilometres divided by a time in minutes gives a meaningless number unless you convert first. Always convert the time into the unit the speed needs before you divide.

To convert between the two speeds, remember that 1 metre per second equals 3.6 kilometres per hour. The reason is simple: 1 metre per second means 3,600 metres in an hour, and 3,600 metres is 3.6 kilometres. So to turn m/s into km/h, multiply by 3.6; to turn km/h into m/s, divide by 3.6. A speed of 20 m/s is 20 × 3.6 = 72 km/h, and a speed of 72 km/h is 72 ÷ 3.6 = 20 m/s. The same pair of numbers will keep appearing in the worked examples below.

Worked example 1: finding the speed of a car

A car travels 240 kilometres in 3 hours. How fast is it going? Use speed = distance ÷ time: 240 ÷ 3 = 80 kilometres per hour. The check is to work backwards: at 80 km/h for 3 hours the car covers 80 × 3 = 240 kilometres, which matches the journey. Always do this reverse check when you can: if distance = speed × time gives back your starting distance, the speed is right.

Now the same journey with minutes in the way. Suppose the car covers 30 kilometres in 45 minutes. First convert the time: 45 minutes is 45 ÷ 60 = 0.75 hours. Then speed = 30 ÷ 0.75 = 40 kilometres per hour. The common error is to divide 30 by 45 and answer 0.67, which has no meaning — 45 is minutes, not hours. Converting first is the whole skill.

Worked example 2: finding the time of a train

A train must cover 150 kilometres at a steady 75 kilometres per hour. How long does the journey take? Use time = distance ÷ speed: 150 ÷ 75 = 2 hours. Again the check works backwards: at 75 km/h for 2 hours the train covers 75 × 2 = 150 kilometres, which matches the distance.

Try a time that is not a whole number of hours. A cyclist rides 10 kilometres at 30 kilometres per hour. Time = 10 ÷ 30 = 1/3 of an hour, which is 1/3 × 60 = 20 minutes. Leaving the answer as a fraction of an hour is correct but unhelpful; converting to minutes makes it something you can actually use. If the division gives a decimal, such as 2.5 hours, that is 2 hours and 0.5 × 60 = 30 minutes, so 2 hours 30 minutes.

Worked example 3: finding the distance of a cyclist

A cyclist rides at a steady 24 kilometres per hour for 2.5 hours. How far does she travel? Use distance = speed × time: 24 × 2.5 = 60 kilometres. The check works backwards again: 60 kilometres in 2.5 hours is 60 ÷ 2.5 = 24 kilometres per hour, which matches the speed.

Watch the units here too. A runner covers 100 metres in 12.5 seconds. Her speed is 100 ÷ 12.5 = 8 metres per second. Convert that to kilometres per hour by multiplying by 3.6: 8 × 3.6 = 28.8 km/h. So the same run is 8 m/s or 28.8 km/h — two ways of saying the same speed, and the conversion is what links them.

Average speed: the trap of the return journey

Average speed is total distance divided by total time, and it is not the same as the average of two speeds. A driver travels 120 kilometres to a town at 60 km/h, which takes 120 ÷ 60 = 2 hours, then drives the same 120 kilometres back at 40 km/h, which takes 120 ÷ 40 = 3 hours. The total distance is 120 + 120 = 240 kilometres and the total time is 2 + 3 = 5 hours, so the average speed for the whole trip is 240 ÷ 5 = 48 kilometres per hour.

The tempting wrong answer is the plain average of 60 and 40, which is 50 km/h. That is wrong because the slower leg takes longer — 3 hours at 40 km/h against 2 hours at 60 km/h — so the slow speed weighs more heavily on the trip. Whenever a journey has legs at different speeds, add up all the distances and all the times first, then divide once. The guide to how averages work explains why an average must always weight each part by how much of it there is.

Common mistakes to avoid

1. Dividing by minutes instead of hours. A speed in km/h needs the time in hours. Convert minutes first: 45 minutes is 0.75 hours, 20 minutes is 1/3 of an hour, 90 minutes is 1.5 hours. Dividing kilometres by minutes gives a number that looks like an answer and means nothing.

2. Mixing kilometres and metres. A distance in metres with a speed in km/h is the same trap in a different coat. Convert first: 500 metres is 0.5 kilometres, and 2 kilometres is 2,000 metres. Pick one distance unit and keep it through the whole calculation.

3. Averaging speeds instead of dividing totals. The return-journey example shows why: the average speed over a whole trip is total distance ÷ total time, not the average of the speeds. The slower leg lasts longer, so it pulls the average down more than a plain average allows.

4. Dividing the wrong way round. Speed is distance ÷ time, not time ÷ distance. If your "speed" comes out as a small decimal like 0.0125, you have probably divided time by distance. Flip it, then check by working backwards: speed × time must give the distance back.

5. Assuming the speed never changes. The formulas give the average speed over a journey, not the speed at every moment. A car that averages 80 km/h over 3 hours will have gone faster and slower along the way — through towns, at traffic lights, on open road. Only a steady speed makes the average equal to the speed at each moment.

Frequently asked questions

How do I convert km/h to m/s? Divide by 3.6. A car at 90 km/h is travelling at 90 ÷ 3.6 = 25 metres per second. To go the other way, multiply by 3.6: 25 m/s × 3.6 = 90 km/h. The 3.6 comes from 3,600 seconds in an hour divided by 1,000 metres in a kilometre.

What if the time is given in minutes? Convert it to hours first, because the speed is usually in kilometres per hour. Divide the minutes by 60: 20 minutes is 20 ÷ 60 = 1/3 of an hour, so 10 kilometres in 20 minutes is 10 ÷ (1/3) = 30 kilometres per hour. Or convert the speed instead: work in kilometres per minute and multiply by 60 at the end.

Can speed be zero or negative? Zero, yes: a parked car has a speed of 0. Negative speed is not used in everyday maths — speed is how fast, and how fast is never below zero. Direction belongs to velocity, a separate idea in physics: velocity is speed with a direction attached, so it can be negative when the direction reverses.

What is the difference between speed and velocity? Speed is a single number: how fast something moves. Velocity adds direction: 80 km/h towards the north is a velocity, 80 km/h on its own is a speed. For everyday journey calculations, speed is all you need; velocity matters when the direction of travel changes.

Speed, distance and time reward one habit above all: sort the units before you divide. Convert minutes to hours, metres to kilometres, and check the answer by working backwards — speed × time must give the distance, distance ÷ speed must give the time. With that habit, any journey becomes three numbers in a triangle, and any one of them follows from the other two.
