How to Work With Negative Numbers — A Complete Guide

Minus five degrees. A bank balance of −£25. A lift that goes down to floor −1. Negative numbers are everywhere, and they follow rules that feel strange at first: subtracting a negative makes a number bigger, and two negatives multiplied together make a positive. This guide explains the number line, the four rules for adding, subtracting, multiplying and dividing negatives, and the mistakes that catch nearly everyone — with worked examples you can check line by line.

The number line: where negative numbers live

Picture a line with zero in the middle. Positive numbers run to the right — 1, 2, 3 — and negative numbers run to the left — −1, −2, −3. The further left a number sits, the smaller it is: −8 is smaller than −3, because −8 is further from zero on the left. That is the first surprise for many people: a bigger-looking number can be smaller. −8 has a bigger digit than −3, but it is the smaller number, in the same way that owing £8 is worse than owing £3.

The number line also makes the opposite of a number easy to see. The opposite of 5 is −5, and the opposite of −5 is 5 — the two numbers sit the same distance from zero on opposite sides. The distance itself has a name: the absolute value. The absolute value of −7 is 7, written |−7| = 7, and the absolute value of 7 is also 7. Temperature is the clearest everyday example: −5°C is five degrees below freezing, and 5°C is five degrees above it — the same distance from zero, on opposite sides. The guide to how fractions, decimals and percentages relate covers the same idea on the number line: every number has a place, and the place tells you its size.

Adding and subtracting negative numbers

Adding a negative number is the same as subtracting: 7 + (−4) = 7 − 4 = 3. Think of it as a debt: you have £7 and you owe £4, so you are left with £3. Adding two negatives makes a bigger negative: (−3) + (−5) = −8, because two debts add up — you owe £3 and you owe £5, so you owe £8. And adding a positive to a negative is a tug of war: (−7) + 4 = −3, because the 7 of debt beats the 4 of credit by 3.

Subtracting a negative is the rule that surprises everyone: two minus signs make a plus. 5 − (−3) = 5 + 3 = 8. Taking away a debt of £3 is the same as gaining £3. The same rule works in reverse: (−4) − (−6) = −4 + 6 = 2, because removing a £6 debt from a £4 debt leaves £2 of credit. The everyday check is temperature: the difference between −5°C and 3°C is 3 − (−5) = 3 + 5 = 8 degrees. Count it on the number line — from −5 up to 0 is 5 steps, and from 0 to 3 is 3 more, giving 8 altogether. The guide to the order of operations matters here too: brackets come first, so work out what is inside them before deciding which sign rule applies.

Worked example 1: a bank balance below zero

A bank account shows −£25, which means the account is £25 overdrawn. A salary payment of £40 arrives. The new balance is (−25) + 40 = 40 − 25 = £15. The account is £15 in credit, and the debt is gone. If instead only £20 arrived, the balance would be (−25) + 20 = −£5 — still £5 overdrawn, because the payment was smaller than the debt.

Now suppose the account holds £30 and a bill of £45 is paid automatically. The new balance is 30 − 45 = −£15. Subtracting a bigger number from a smaller one always gives a negative answer: 30 − 45 is the same as −(45 − 30) = −15. The check on the number line: start at 30, move 45 steps left, and you land at −15 — 30 steps take you to zero, and the remaining 15 steps take you to −15. Money below zero is the most common place people meet negative numbers, and the rule is simple: a negative balance is a debt, and every payment first pays off the debt.

Worked example 2: multiplying and dividing — the sign rules

Multiplication with negatives follows two rules: same signs give a positive answer, different signs give a negative answer. A positive times a positive is positive: 3 × 4 = 12. A negative times a positive is negative: (−3) × 4 = −12 — three groups of a £4 debt is a £12 debt. A positive times a negative is also negative: 5 × (−2) = −10. And a negative times a negative is positive: (−3) × (−4) = 12. The last one is the hardest to accept, but the pattern proves it: 3 × (−4) = −12, 2 × (−4) = −8, 1 × (−4) = −4, 0 × (−4) = 0, so (−1) × (−4) must be 4, and (−3) × (−4) must be 12 — each step adds 4, and the sequence crosses zero exactly where the rule says it should.

Division follows the same sign rules. (−12) ÷ 3 = −4, because a negative divided by a positive is negative. 12 ÷ (−3) = −4, for the same reason — the signs differ. And (−12) ÷ (−3) = 4, because the signs match. The check is multiplication in reverse: −4 × 3 = −12, so (−12) ÷ 3 = −4 is right; and 4 × (−3) = −12, so (−12) ÷ (−3) = 4 is right. If you ever forget whether the answer is positive or negative, count the minus signs: an even number of them (0 or 2) gives a positive answer, and an odd number (1) gives a negative one.

Worked example 3: ordering and comparing negatives

Put these temperatures in order from coldest to warmest: 2°C, −8°C, −3°C, 0°C. On the number line, the coldest is the furthest left, so the order is −8°C, −3°C, 0°C, 2°C. The inequality signs tell the same story: −8 < −3 < 0 < 2. The common error is to order them by the size of the digits — 8, 3, 0, 2 — which puts −3 before −8 and gets the answer backwards. Always place the numbers on the line first: left means smaller, right means bigger, whatever the digits say.

The same skill settles sports and money questions. A golfer is 3 under par (−3) and another is 1 over par (+1): the first golfer is winning, because −3 is smaller than +1. A shop that loses £200 in January (−200) and gains £150 in February (+150) is £50 down over the two months: (−200) + 150 = −50. The guide to how averages work shows what happens next: the mean of −200 and +150 is (−200 + 150) ÷ 2 = −50 ÷ 2 = −25, so the shop lost an average of £25 a month — a negative average, which simply means an average loss.

Common mistakes to avoid

1. Thinking −8 is bigger than −3. The digit is bigger, but the number is smaller, because −8 sits further left on the number line. Owing £8 is worse than owing £3. Always compare by position on the line, not by the size of the digits.

2. Treating subtracting a negative as subtracting. 5 − (−3) is 8, not 2. The two minus signs cancel into a plus. Read it as "take away a debt of three" and the answer follows: removing a debt makes you richer, not poorer.

3. Getting the sign wrong in multiplication. (−3) × (−4) is +12, not −12. Same signs, positive answer; different signs, negative answer. Count the minus signs if you are unsure: two of them means positive.

4. Forgetting the sign when adding a positive to a negative. (−7) + 4 is −3, not −11 and not 11. The bigger number wins the tug of war, and the answer takes its sign: 7 beats 4, so the answer is negative; the gap is 7 − 4 = 3.

5. Dropping the minus sign in the middle of a calculation. In a long sum such as 6 − 9 + 4, the answer is 1, not 11 and not −1: 6 − 9 = −3, then −3 + 4 = 1. Write the sign with every number, work left to right, and check the final sign by asking whether the answer should be above or below zero.

Frequently asked questions

What is the opposite of a negative number? Its positive twin, the same distance from zero on the other side of the number line. The opposite of −5 is 5, and the opposite of 5 is −5. Taking the opposite twice brings you back: the opposite of the opposite of −5 is −5 again.

Can you take the square root of a negative number? Not with ordinary numbers. No ordinary number multiplied by itself gives a negative answer, because same signs give a positive result — 4 × 4 = 16 and (−4) × (−4) = 16. So √(−16) has no ordinary answer. (Mathematicians use imaginary numbers for this, but that is beyond everyday maths.)

Why is minus times minus a plus? Because the pattern demands it: 3 × (−4) = −12, 2 × (−4) = −8, 1 × (−4) = −4, 0 × (−4) = 0, so each step down adds 4, and (−1) × (−4) must be 4. The rule keeps the pattern unbroken. In money terms, removing a debt is a gain — and multiplying by −1 twice removes the removal.

Where do negative numbers appear in real life? Everywhere a quantity can fall below zero: temperatures below freezing, bank balances below zero, floors below ground level, golf scores under par, and heights below sea level — the Dead Sea shore sits about 430 metres below sea level, written as −430 metres. Any scale with a zero in the middle has negatives on one side.

Negative numbers stop being strange once the number line does the work: left is smaller, right is bigger, and every operation is a move along the line. Adding a negative moves left, subtracting a negative moves right, and multiplication and division follow the sign rules — same signs positive, different signs negative. With those rules, a temperature of −5°C, a balance of −£25 and a lift to floor −1 are all the same idea: a number on the other side of zero.
