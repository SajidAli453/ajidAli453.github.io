How to Calculate a Tip Quickly — A Complete Guide

The bill arrives, the meal was good, and now everyone at the table is doing the same quiet maths in their head: how much is 18% of $84? A tip is just a percentage of the bill, and once you know one trick — find 10% first — every common tip rate becomes a few seconds of mental arithmetic. This guide shows the 10% trick, how to build 15%, 20% and 25% from it, and how to split a tip between friends.

What a tip actually is

A tip is a percentage of the bill, paid on top of it. If the bill is $60 and you tip 20%, the tip is 20% of $60, which is $12, and the total you pay is $60 + $12 = $72. The rule is simple: tip = bill × percentage ÷ 100, so 15% of a $40 bill is $6.00 and the total is $46.00. The formula never changes; only the percentage does. In the United States the usual range is 15% to 20% for table service, with 20% the most common for good service. In many other countries 10% is normal, or people simply round the bill up. The maths is the same everywhere — what changes is the percentage you choose.

Tipping feels hard because 15% and 18% do not divide neatly in your head — so never calculate them directly. Find 10% first, then build the answer from it.

The 10% trick: move the decimal point

To find 10% of any amount, move the decimal point one place to the left. That is all there is to it. 10% of $48 is $4.80. 10% of $64.50 is $6.45. 10% of $120 is $12.00. 10% of $23.60 is $2.36. The decimal point moves one step left and the answer appears. If the bill is a whole number, imagine the decimal point at the end: 10% of $75 is $7.50, because $75.00 with the point moved one step left is $7.50.

Why does it work? Because 10% means ten out of a hundred, which is the same as one tenth — and dividing by 10 moves the decimal point one place left. That is the same rule the guide to how to calculate a percentage uses. Once you have 10%, you have the building block for every other tip rate: 20% is double it, 5% is half of it, and 15% is 10% plus 5%.

Building 15%, 20% and 25% from 10%

Take a $48 bill. 10% is $4.80. To find 20%, double it: $4.80 × 2 = $9.60. To find 5%, halve it: $4.80 ÷ 2 = $2.40. To find 15%, add the two pieces: $4.80 + $2.40 = $7.20. Three answers from one starting number, and none of them needed a calculator. The same steps work on any bill: find 10%, then double it for 20%, halve it for 5%, and add them for 15%.

25% is even simpler, because 25% is a quarter of the bill: divide the bill by 4. 25% of $48 is $48 ÷ 4 = $12.00, and 25% of $60 is $15.00. The guide to how discounts and sale prices work uses the same idea in reverse: a discount is a percentage taken off, while a tip is a percentage added on.

Worked example 1: a $36.40 dinner at 15%

The bill is $36.40 and you want to leave 15%. Start with 10%: move the decimal point one place left, so 10% of $36.40 is $3.64. Now find 5%, which is half of 10%: $3.64 ÷ 2 = $1.82. Add them: $3.64 + $1.82 = $5.46. The tip is $5.46, and the total you pay is $36.40 + $5.46 = $41.86. Check it by the formula: 36.40 × 15 ÷ 100 = 546 ÷ 100 = $5.46 — the same answer.

If $5.46 feels fussy, round the tip to $5.50 and pay $41.90 in total. Rounding the tip to the nearest 50 cents is normal and nobody checks the exact cents — the point of the 10% trick is to get close quickly, then round to a tidy number.

Worked example 2: an $84 bill at 18%

Eighteen percent is the rate that makes people reach for their phone, but it is still just 10% plus 5% plus 3%. The bill is $84. 10% is $8.40. 5% is half of that: $4.20. 3% is a little harder, so find 1% first — move the decimal point two places left, so 1% of $84 is $0.84 — and multiply by 3: $0.84 × 3 = $2.52. Now add the three pieces: $8.40 + $4.20 + $2.52 = $15.12. The tip is $15.12, and the total is $84 + $15.12 = $99.12.

Check it another way: 20% of $84 is $16.80, and 2% of $84 is $1.68, so 18% is $16.80 − $1.68 = $15.12 — the same answer again.

Worked example 3: splitting the tip four ways

Four friends share a $120 bill and want to leave 15%. There are two ways to do it, and they agree. First, find the tip on the whole bill: 10% of $120 is $12.00, 5% is $6.00, so 15% is $18.00. The total is $120 + $18 = $138, and each person pays $138 ÷ 4 = $34.50. Second, split the bill first and tip on each share: $120 ÷ 4 = $30 each, 15% of $30 is $3.00 + $1.50 = $4.50, so each person pays $30 + $4.50 = $34.50. Both routes give $34.50 per person, as they must — the maths is the same, only the order changes.

When the bill does not divide evenly, split the total instead of the tip. A $95 bill with a 20% tip is $95 + $19 = $114, and $114 ÷ 3 = $38 each — clean. Splitting the bill first would give $95 ÷ 3 = $31.67 each, and 20% of that is $6.33 — the same $38.00 after rounding, reached with messier numbers. The guide to how to split a bill fairly covers the cases where people ordered different things; for an even split, tip the whole bill first, then divide.

Rounding: when close enough is fine

Exact cents matter on a receipt, but not in your head. A $52 bill at 20% gives a tip of $10.40 and a total of $62.40 — and most people would simply leave $10 or $11 and pay $62 or $63. A $47.50 bill at 20% gives a tip of exactly $9.50, which is already tidy. The rule of thumb is to round the tip to the nearest dollar on a large bill and to the nearest 50 cents on a small one, and to round up rather than down when the service was good: the difference is a few coins, and rounding up is the generous direction.

Rounding works because the 10% trick already gives you the exact answer — you round a number you have already found, instead of guessing one you have not. If the exact 15% tip on a $63 bill is $9.45, leaving $9.50 is a rounding choice, not an error. But round the tip, not the bill: 15% of $60 is $9.00, so rounding the bill down by $3 costs the server 45 cents.

Tip on the pre-tax bill or the total?

In places where tax is added to the bill, there are two numbers you could tip on: the food and drink total before tax, or the full total after tax. The usual advice is to tip on the pre-tax amount, because tax is not service. Take a bill of $80 with $6.40 of tax, making $86.40 in total. 20% on the pre-tax $80 is $16.00. 20% on the full $86.40 is $17.28 — $1.28 more. Over a year of eating out, that difference adds up, and it is money the server did not earn by serving you.

In practice many people tip on the total because it is the number printed largest on the receipt, and the difference is small. Neither way is wrong. But the standard answer is the pre-tax amount: find the subtotal, move the decimal point for 10%, and build from there.

Common mistakes to avoid

1. Moving the decimal point the wrong way. 10% of $40 is $4.00, not $0.40. Moving the point one place left makes the number smaller, but only by one place: $40 becomes $4.00. If your 10% answer looks ten times too small, you moved the point two places instead of one.

2. Tipping on the tax. Tax is not part of the service, so the usual rule is to tip on the pre-tax subtotal. On an $80 bill with $6.40 of tax, 20% on the pre-tax amount is $16.00, while 20% on the total is $17.28 — a $1.28 difference on one meal.

3. Mixing up 15% with $15. A 15% tip on a $40 bill is $6.00, not $15. The percentage always depends on the bill: 15% of $100 is $15, but 15% of $40 is only $6.00. Always multiply the percentage by the bill — never use the percentage number as a dollar amount.

4. Forgetting to add the tip to the total. A $60 bill with a 20% tip is $72 to pay, not $60. The tip is extra, on top of the bill. When you pay by card, the receipt shows the bill and a blank tip line — the total you write is bill plus tip, so $60 + $12 = $72.

5. Doubling the wrong number for 20%. 20% is double 10%, so on a $64.50 bill, 10% is $6.45 and 20% is $12.90. The mistake is doubling the bill instead: $64.50 × 2 = $129 is the bill doubled, not the tip. Always double the small number.

Frequently asked questions

How much should I tip? In the United States, 15% to 20% of the pre-tax bill is standard for table service, with 20% the most common for good service. In the United Kingdom and much of Europe, 10% is usual, or people round the bill up, and a service charge is often already included — check the receipt before adding anything. In many other countries tipping is not expected at all.

What if a service charge is already on the bill? Then the tip is already included, and you do not need to add one. Look for "service charge", "gratuity" or "service included" on the receipt. If a 12.5% service charge is already there, adding 15% more means you have tipped 27.5% — check first.

Do I tip on takeaway or delivery? For delivery, a small tip is usual — often 10% or a few dollars, whichever is larger. For takeaway you collect yourself, tipping is optional and often just the change rounded up: 10% of a $28 delivery order is $2.80, and rounding up to $3 or $4 is a normal delivery tip.

How do I tip when paying by card? The card machine or receipt shows the bill, a blank tip line, and a total line. Work out the tip with the 10% trick, write it on the tip line, and add it to the bill for the total: on a $52 bill with a $10 tip, you write 10.00 and 62.00. If the machine asks for a percentage instead, pick the nearest option — 15%, 18% or 20% — and it does the maths for you.

Tipping is one percentage, found one easy step at a time: move the decimal point one place left for 10%, double it for 20%, halve it for 5%, add the pieces for 15%, and divide by 4 for 25%. Round the tip, not the bill, tip on the pre-tax amount when you can, and check for a service charge first. The bill that used to mean reaching for a phone becomes ten seconds of mental maths — and the answer is right.
