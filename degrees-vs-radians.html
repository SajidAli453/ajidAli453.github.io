Degrees vs Radians Explained — When to Use Each One

Angles can be measured in degrees or in radians, and the two systems describe exactly the same turns, just on different scales. Degrees divide a full circle into 360 equal parts, a system with ancient roots in astronomy and navigation. Radians measure an angle by the length of arc it cuts out of a circle, compared with the radius of that circle. A full circle is 360 degrees, and it is also 2π radians, where π is about 3.14159. That single equivalence — 360 degrees equals 2π radians — is the bridge between the two systems, and everything else follows from it.

What is a radian, in plain terms?

Imagine a circle with radius r. Take an arc along the edge of the circle that is also length r, and draw lines from the centre to each end of that arc. The angle between those lines is one radian. Because the full circumference of a circle is 2πr, there are 2π radians in a full turn, which is about 6.283 radians. A half turn is π radians, a quarter turn is π ÷ 2 radians, and a right angle — 90 degrees — is therefore π ÷ 2 radians, about 1.571 radians. Radians feel strange at first because they are not whole numbers for common angles, but they are natural in mathematics because they come directly from the geometry of the circle, with no arbitrary choice of 360.

Converting between degrees and radians

To convert degrees to radians, multiply by π and divide by 180. For example, 30 degrees becomes 30 × π ÷ 180 = π ÷ 6 radians, about 0.524 radians. To convert radians to degrees, multiply by 180 and divide by π. For example, 1 radian becomes 1 × 180 ÷ π = about 57.3 degrees, a useful fact to remember: one radian is a little under 60 degrees. A table of common conversions helps while you learn: 0 degrees is 0 radians, 30 degrees is π ÷ 6, 45 degrees is π ÷ 4, 60 degrees is π ÷ 3, 90 degrees is π ÷ 2, 180 degrees is π, and 360 degrees is 2π. Notice how the fractions of π make the pattern easy to spot, even if the decimal values look messy.

Why does the calculator have a Deg/Rad switch?

Trigonometric functions like sine, cosine and tangent depend on the angle unit. Sin(30) is 0.5 when 30 means 30 degrees, because in a right-angled triangle with a 30-degree angle, the opposite side is half the hypotenuse. But sin(30) in radians is about −0.988, a completely different number, because 30 radians is many full turns around the circle. This is the single most common calculator mistake in trigonometry, and it is entirely avoidable: check the mode before you calculate, and check it again after someone else has used the calculator. If an answer looks wildly wrong, the mode is the first thing to suspect, before the formula or the input.

When to use degrees, and when to use radians

Degrees are the everyday choice: school geometry, navigation, construction, maps and most practical measuring use degrees, because whole numbers like 30, 45 and 90 are easy to talk about and to set on a protractor or a compass. Radians are the standard choice in higher mathematics, physics and engineering, especially in calculus, circular motion and waves. The reason is that many formulas become simpler in radians: for small angles, sin(x) is approximately x only when x is in radians, and the derivative of sin(x) is cos(x) only in radians. In physics, angular speed is naturally measured in radians per second, because it connects directly to linear speed through the radius. So the rule of thumb is: practical measuring, use degrees; calculus and physics formulas, use radians, unless the problem states otherwise.

Worked examples

Convert 120 degrees to radians: 120 × π ÷ 180 = 2π ÷ 3, about 2.094 radians. Convert π ÷ 4 radians to degrees: π ÷ 4 × 180 ÷ π = 45 degrees. Find cos(60 degrees): 0.5, because the adjacent side in that classic triangle is half the hypotenuse. Find sin(π ÷ 2 radians): 1, because π ÷ 2 is a quarter turn, the same as 90 degrees, where sine reaches its maximum. If you get sin(π ÷ 2) = 0.5 or some other unexpected value, you are almost certainly in degree mode while the input is in radians, or vice versa.

Common mistakes

The biggest mistake is the mode mix-up described above. The second is forgetting that π in a radian measure is not 180 degrees in every context: π radians equals 180 degrees, but π as a number is about 3.14, so 2π radians is about 6.28, not 360, unless you convert. The third is rounding too early: keep π in your working as a symbol until the last step, then convert to a decimal, so small rounding errors do not grow. The fourth is assuming radians are only for advanced students: they are just another unit, like centimetres versus inches, and learning both early makes later maths much less confusing.

A good habit is to write the unit every time: 30° or 30 degrees, and π ÷ 6 rad or 0.524 radians. That one habit prevents most errors, makes your working clear to a teacher or colleague, and reminds you to set the calculator correctly. Once degrees and radians both feel familiar, you can move freely between them, choosing degrees when you are measuring something in the world and radians when a formula or a physics problem asks for them, confident that you are describing the same angle in two different, equally valid languages.
