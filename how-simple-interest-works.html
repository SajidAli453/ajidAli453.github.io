How Simple Interest Works — A Complete Guide

When you borrow money, you usually pay back more than you borrowed. When you save money in a bank account, the bank usually pays you a little extra. That extra amount is interest, and in its simplest form it is a price for time: the longer money is borrowed or saved, the more interest it earns or costs. Simple interest is the most straightforward way of working that price out, and it is the method behind a great many school problems, short-term loans and savings examples.

This guide explains the simple interest formula, what each part of it means, and how to convert percentages and time periods so the numbers line up. It also shows where people most often go wrong, and how simple interest differs from compound interest, so you can tell at a glance which kind of problem you are looking at.

What simple interest means

Simple interest is calculated only on the original amount of money, called the principal. It does not change as interest builds up, because the interest itself never earns further interest. If you borrow 1,000 and pay 5% simple interest a year, you pay the same amount of interest in the third year as in the first year, because the calculation always starts again from the same 1,000.

That steadiness is what makes simple interest simple. The interest grows in equal steps, one step per year, like adding the same amount again and again. After one year you owe one step of interest, after two years two steps, and after three years three steps. There are no surprises, and you can check the answer by counting the steps.

The formula, step by step

The formula is usually written as I = P × r × t. Here, I stands for the interest, P stands for the principal, r stands for the interest rate, and t stands for the time. The total amount to be paid back, or the total value of the savings at the end, is the principal plus the interest, P + I.

The rate, r, must be written as a decimal, not as a percentage. A rate of 5% a year means 5 out of every 100, which is 0.05 as a decimal. A rate of 4% is 0.04, a rate of 12% is 0.12, and a rate of 0.5% is 0.005. Dividing the percentage by 100 does this conversion, and forgetting it is the most common error in the whole topic: using 5 instead of 0.05 makes the answer one hundred times too large.

The time, t, must be in the same unit as the rate. Rates are almost always quoted per year, so t must be measured in years. Eighteen months is 1.5 years, six months is 0.5 years, and three months is 0.25 years. If a problem gives a time in days, divide the number of days by 365 to turn it into a fraction of a year, unless the problem states a different convention. What matters is that the rate and the time agree: a yearly rate with a time in months, used without converting, will give the wrong answer.

Converting the rate and the time

Most mistakes in simple interest are not multiplication mistakes at all. They are unit mistakes, made before the multiplication starts. A rate given as “5% per year” and a time given as “18 months” cannot go straight into the formula together, because one is yearly and the other is monthly. Convert one of them first, and write the conversion down, so you can see it when you check your work.

To convert a percentage to a decimal, divide by 100, which is the same as moving the decimal point two places to the left: 5% becomes 0.05, 7.5% becomes 0.075, and 0.5% becomes 0.005. To convert months to years, divide by 12: 6 months is 0.5 years, 9 months is 0.75 years, and 18 months is 1.5 years. The guide to calculating a percentage and the Percentage Calculator can help if the rate itself has to be worked out from two amounts first.

Worked example 1: interest on a loan

Someone borrows 1,000 at 5% simple interest a year for 3 years. The principal, P, is 1,000, the rate, r, is 0.05, and the time, t, is 3. The interest is I = 1,000 × 0.05 × 3, which is 150. The total to pay back is the principal plus the interest, 1,000 + 150, which is 1,150.

Counting in steps gives the same answer, which is a useful check. Each year adds 5% of 1,000, which is 50. After one year the interest is 50, after two years it is 100, and after three years it is 150. The equal steps are the signature of simple interest: the same 50 every year, because the principal never changes.

Worked example 2: a time given in months

Someone saves 2,500 at 4% simple interest a year for 18 months. First, convert the time: 18 months divided by 12 is 1.5 years. The principal is 2,500, the rate is 0.04, and the time is 1.5. The interest is I = 2,500 × 0.04 × 1.5, which is 150, and the savings are worth 2,500 + 150, which is 2,650 at the end.

Notice that this example gives the same interest as the first one, 150, even though the amounts and rates are different. That is a coincidence, not a rule, and it is worth pausing over: 2,500 at 4% for 1.5 years happens to equal 1,000 at 5% for 3 years, because 2,500 × 0.04 × 1.5 and 1,000 × 0.05 × 3 both come to 150. The formula only cares about the product of the three numbers, so different combinations can land on the same interest.

Worked example 3: working backwards to find the rate

Sometimes the interest is known and the rate is the missing piece. Someone borrows 2,000 for 2 years and pays 240 in simple interest. What was the yearly rate? Rearranging the formula gives r = I ÷ (P × t), so r = 240 ÷ (2,000 × 2), which is 240 ÷ 4,000, which is 0.06. As a percentage, that is 6% a year.

The same rearrangement works for the other missing pieces. To find the principal, use P = I ÷ (r × t). To find the time, use t = I ÷ (P × r). In each case, keep the units straight first: the rate as a decimal, the time in years. If the answer comes out as a decimal for the rate, multiply by 100 to turn it back into a percentage for the final answer.

Simple interest and compound interest: the key difference

Compound interest is calculated on the principal plus the interest already earned, so the amount grows faster over time. Simple interest is calculated on the principal alone, so it grows in equal steps. The difference is small over one year, because in the first year both methods give the same result, but it grows with every year that passes.

Take 1,000 at 10% a year for 2 years. With simple interest, each year adds 10% of 1,000, which is 100, so the interest after 2 years is 200 and the total is 1,200. With compound interest, the first year adds 100, making 1,100, and the second year adds 10% of 1,100, which is 110, so the interest after 2 years is 210 and the total is 1,210. The extra 10 is interest earned on the first year's interest, which simple interest never charges. If a problem says “compounded annually” or “interest is added to the balance”, it is not a simple interest problem, and this formula will understate the answer.

Common mistakes to avoid

Using the percentage number instead of the decimal is the biggest trap: 5 must become 0.05 before it goes into the formula. Mixing up the time units is the second: a yearly rate needs a time in years, so months must be divided by 12 first. A third mistake is giving the interest as the final answer when the question asked for the total amount to pay back, or the other way round: interest, I, and total, P + I, are different numbers, and the question will usually ask for one of them by name.

A subtler mistake is assuming that a rate quoted per month is the same as a rate quoted per year. A rate of 1% a month is roughly 12% a year under simple interest, not 1% a year, and treating it as yearly understates the interest by a factor of twelve. Always read the words after the rate: “per year”, “per annum” and “p.a.” all mean yearly, while “per month” means the time must be counted in months, or the rate converted to a yearly one first.

Frequently asked questions

Is the answer the interest or the total to pay back?

The formula gives the interest, I. If the question asks how much is paid back in total, or what the savings are worth at the end, add the principal: total = P + I. In the first example above, the interest is 150 and the total to pay back is 1,150, and those are answers to two different questions.

What if the time is given in months or days?

Convert it to years before using the formula, because the rate is almost always yearly. Divide months by 12 and days by 365, unless the problem gives a different convention, such as a 360-day year. Write the converted time down before multiplying, so a unit mistake is easy to spot.

Can the rate change during the loan?

Not within a single simple interest calculation: the formula assumes one fixed rate for the whole period. If the rate changes part-way through, split the period into two parts, work out the interest for each part at its own rate, and add the two amounts together. Each part still uses only the original principal, because that is what makes it simple interest.

Is simple interest used in real life, or only in school problems?

Both. It is the standard model for many short-term loans and some instalment and savings products, and it is how interest is often quoted in everyday comparisons. Many longer-term products, including most mortgages and credit cards, use compound interest or an amortisation schedule instead, so the simple interest answer is a starting point for comparison, not a substitute for reading the actual terms. For any real loan, check whether interest is simple or compound before relying on the figure.
