Fractions in the Kitchen — Halving and Doubling Recipes

A recipe serves four people, and two are coming to dinner. It calls for 3/4 cup of flour, 1/2 cup of sugar, 2 eggs and 1 teaspoon of baking powder, and the cook needs exactly half of each amount. Halving 2 eggs is easy. Halving 3/4 cup of flour is a fraction problem: 3/4 times 1/2 is 3/8 of a cup, which is 6 tablespoons. No measuring cup makes that amount easy to judge, so the cook needs the fraction arithmetic and the spoon conversions that turn an awkward fraction into a measure that can actually be scooped from a jar.

This guide explains why recipes are written in fractions, how to scale any recipe with a single scale factor, and which fraction skills the job needs: multiplying, simplifying, turning improper fractions into mixed numbers, and converting between cups, tablespoons and teaspoons. It works through three examples — halving, scaling four servings to six, and doubling — and shows the mistakes that most often spoil a scaled recipe. The measures follow the cup convention used in most printed and online recipes, where 1 cup equals 16 tablespoons.

Why fractions matter in cooking

Recipes are written in fractions because cooks measure by volume with cups and spoons, and cups and spoons divide naturally into halves, thirds and quarters. A recipe does not call for 0.375 cups of flour, because no measuring cup is marked in thousandths. It calls for 3/8 cup, or more often for 6 tablespoons — the same amount in an easier measure. Fractions are the language of the measuring cup, and a cook who can work with them can adjust any recipe to the people actually eating.

Scaling matters because the servings in a recipe rarely match the people at the table. Cooking the full recipe for half the people wastes food, and guessing at each amount changes the balance of the dish: too much baking powder for the flour makes a cake rise and then collapse, and too little salt makes the whole dish taste flat. Keeping every ingredient in the same proportion is what keeps the recipe working, and fractions preserve that proportion when the quantity changes.

The core method: one scale factor for every ingredient

Every scaling job uses the same three steps. First, work out the scale factor: divide the servings wanted by the servings in the recipe. Second, multiply every ingredient by that scale factor, with no exceptions. Third, simplify each answer and, where the result is awkward in cups, convert it into tablespoons or teaspoons. The scale factor is one fraction applied to every ingredient alike, because a recipe is a set of proportions, not a list of independent amounts.

Halving uses a scale factor of 1/2, and doubling uses 2. Awkward serving counts use a fraction in between: a recipe for 4 servings cooked for 6 people needs 6 divided by 4, which simplifies to 3/2, or 1 1/2. In percentage terms, halving is 50 percent, doubling is 200 percent, and scaling from 4 to 6 is 150 percent — the same calculation, though the fraction is usually easier. The factor must apply to every ingredient: scaling the flour but leaving the baking powder unchanged changes the ratio the recipe depends on.

The fraction skills you need

Multiplying fractions follows one rule: multiply the top numbers together, and multiply the bottom numbers together. To multiply 3/4 by 1/2, multiply 3 by 1 to get 3, and 4 by 2 to get 8, giving 3/8. A whole number is treated as a fraction with 1 underneath it, so doubling 2/3 cup means multiplying 2/3 by 2/1, which gives 4/3. The same rule explains a neat result below: 2/3 times 3/2 is 6/6, which is 1 — so scaling a 2/3 cup measure by 3/2 lands exactly on 1 cup.

Simplifying means dividing the top and bottom numbers by the same number until no further division is possible: 4/6 simplifies to 2/3, and 10/16 to 5/8. Simplifying does not change the amount; it writes it in the form a cook can recognise, and 6/6 should always be written as 1. The third skill is turning an improper fraction — top number larger than the bottom — into a mixed number: divide 9 by 4 to get 2 with 1 left over, so 9/4 is 2 1/4, and likewise 5/2 is 2 1/2 and 7/4 is 1 3/4. Mixed numbers are how scaled recipes are usually written, because 2 1/4 cups is far easier to measure than 9/4 cups.

The fourth skill is converting between cups, tablespoons and teaspoons, because fractions of a cup are often easier to measure in spoons. One cup equals 16 tablespoons, one tablespoon equals 3 teaspoons, and one cup therefore equals 48 teaspoons. The useful landmarks are 1/2 cup, which is 8 tablespoons; 1/4 cup, which is 4 tablespoons; 1/8 cup, which is 2 tablespoons; and 3/8 cup, which is 6 tablespoons. Half a tablespoon is 1 1/2 teaspoons. Thirds are awkward: 1/3 cup is 16 teaspoons (5 tablespoons plus 1 teaspoon), and half of that is 8 teaspoons (2 tablespoons plus 2 teaspoons). For small amounts, work in teaspoons; between an eighth and half a cup, work in tablespoons.

Worked example 1: halving a recipe

A recipe serves four and uses 3/4 cup of flour, 1/2 cup of sugar, 2 eggs and 1 teaspoon of baking powder. Cooking for two means halving it, so the scale factor is 1/2. The flour becomes 3/4 times 1/2, which is 3/8 cup — and 3/8 times 16 tablespoons is 6 tablespoons, the measure to actually use. The sugar becomes 1/2 times 1/2, which is 1/4 cup, or 4 tablespoons. The eggs become 2 times 1/2, which is 1 egg, and the baking powder becomes 1 times 1/2, which is 1/2 teaspoon.

Every step follows the same pattern: multiply, simplify, and convert to a usable measure. The flour needed the conversion, because 3/8 cup is hard to measure directly; stopping there and guessing would very likely give too much or too little flour.

Worked example 2: scaling four servings to six

A recipe serves four and uses 2/3 cup of milk, 1 1/2 cups of flour, 4 eggs and 3/4 teaspoon of salt. Cooking for six needs 6 divided by 4, which simplifies to 3/2. The milk becomes 2/3 times 3/2, which is 6/6, or exactly 1 cup. The flour becomes 1 1/2 times 3/2 — write 1 1/2 as 3/2, giving 9/4, or 2 1/4 cups. The eggs become 4 times 3/2, which is 6.

The salt becomes 3/4 times 3/2: multiply 3 by 3 to get 9, and 4 by 2 to get 8, giving 9/8, which is 1 1/8 teaspoons. That measure is too small for ordinary spoons, so use 1 teaspoon plus a pinch. The arithmetic gives the exact amount; the cook then chooses the nearest usable measure, keeping the error as small as possible.

Worked example 3: doubling a recipe

A recipe uses 2/3 cup of sugar, 1 1/2 teaspoons of vanilla extract, 3/4 cup of butter and 5 tablespoons of oil. Doubling uses a scale factor of 2. The sugar becomes 2/3 times 2, which is 4/3, or 1 1/3 cups. The vanilla becomes 1 1/2 times 2: write 1 1/2 as 3/2, multiply by 2 to get 6/2, which is 3 teaspoons, and 3 teaspoons equal 1 tablespoon. The butter becomes 3/4 times 2, which is 6/4, simplifying to 3/2, or 1 1/2 cups.

The oil becomes 5 times 2, which is 10 tablespoons. Dividing by 16 gives 10/16, or 5/8 cup, though measuring 10 tablespoons is straightforward. The vanilla is the result to remember: one tablespoon replaces three teaspoon measures with no loss of accuracy.

Common mistakes to avoid

Scaling only some ingredients is the most common error, and the most damaging, because it changes the proportions the recipe depends on. The usual victims are the small amounts — salt, baking powder, spices — which feel too small to matter but are scaled for the same reason as the flour. A second mistake is treating eggs and spices as if they always scale like flour and sugar: an odd number of eggs halved gives half an egg, handled by beating the egg and using half of it, and a doubled strong spice can overpower a dish, so spices are best scaled, tasted and adjusted rather than doubled blindly.

Three more traps are worth watching. Mixing up 1/3 and 1/2 is easy when reading quickly, but they differ by 1/6 of the whole measure — a large error in a small recipe, since 1/2 equals 3/6 and 1/3 equals 2/6. Rounding fractions too early is another: keep fractions exact until the final measure and round only once, at the end. Finally, adding the scale factor instead of multiplying by it — adding 1/2 cup rather than multiplying every amount by 1/2 — is not scaling at all.

Frequently asked questions

What if a recipe serves four and I need five servings?

Use a scale factor of 5 divided by 4, which is 5/4, or 1 1/4. Two cups of flour become 2 times 5/4, which is 10/4, or 2 1/2 cups. A measure of 3/4 cup becomes 15/16 cup — one tablespoon short of a full cup, since a full cup is 48 teaspoons and 15/16 cup is 45 teaspoons, so use a full cup minus 1 tablespoon. The method is the same for any serving count: servings wanted divided by servings given, then multiply everything by that fraction.

How do I halve a recipe that uses one egg?

Beat the egg until yolk and white are mixed, then use half of the beaten egg — there is no cleaner way to measure half an egg. The same approach works for three eggs halved, which gives 1 1/2 eggs: beat three eggs and use half the mixture, keeping the proportion correct. Do not leave the egg out: it is part of the recipe's structure as well as its quantity.

Do cooking times change when a recipe is scaled?

No. Doubling a recipe does not double the cooking time, and halving it does not halve the time, because heat reaches food from the outside in and the size and shape of the dish matter as much as the quantity. A larger quantity usually needs somewhat longer, but the only reliable guide is to check the food earlier than the original time and keep checking. The fractions set the quantities; the timing is judged by looking at the food.

What is half of 1/3 cup?

Half of 1/3 cup is 1/6 cup, because 1/3 times 1/2 is 1/6. One-sixth of a cup is 8 teaspoons — 2 tablespoons plus 2 teaspoons — and that spoon measure is the practical way to use it. A third of a cup is itself 16 teaspoons, so halving it in teaspoons (16 halved is 8) is often easier than working with the fraction directly. Whenever a fraction of a cup looks unmeasurable, convert to teaspoons first, then convert back if the result is a whole number of spoonfuls.
